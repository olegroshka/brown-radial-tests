A self-contained reader's guide to the paper "Beyond Brown's radial tests"

Brown's undecided zone, and what decides it

A theorem from 1971 turns a question about estimators (can this one be beaten?) into a question about a wandering particle (does it ever come home for good?). Brown gave two tests for the particle; both can fall silent at once. This guide builds up everything the paper relies on, from Gaussian blur to weighted Brownian motion to the electrical meaning of "coming home", then states the paper's results and shows what they are good for. It assumes first university courses in calculus, linear algebra and probability, and explains everything else on the way.

  2. Random motion and weighted spaces: random walks, three kinds of Brownian motion, weighted spaces, coming home in electrical language

Part I

The statistical question

1. Measuring many things at once

Suppose you measure \(m\) quantities, each once, and each measurement carries Gaussian noise of unit variance. The offsets of \(m\) sensors on a calibration bench; the positions of \(m\) stars on one exposure; the "true" batting abilities of \(m\) baseball players seen through a few weeks of results. Write the unknown true values as a vector \(\theta\in\mathbb R^m\) and the measurements as

\[ X = \theta + \text{noise}, \qquad X\sim N_m(\theta, I). \]

An estimator is any rule \(\delta(X)\) that turns the measurement into a guess. Its quality at a given \(\theta\) is its risk, the mean squared error averaged over the noise:

\[ R(\theta,\delta) = E_\theta\,|\delta(X)-\theta|^2 . \]

The obvious rule, \(\delta(X)=X\), has risk exactly \(m\) for every \(\theta\): each coordinate contributes its unit noise variance. One rule dominates another if its risk is never larger and is strictly smaller for at least one \(\theta\). A rule that nobody dominates is admissible; a rule that somebody dominates is inadmissible and, in that precise sense, can be beaten. Admissibility is the weakest thing one can ask of an estimator: not that it is best, only that nothing is better everywhere.

In 1956 Stein showed that for \(m\ge3\) the obvious rule \(X\) is inadmissible. James and Stein (1961) wrote down a rule that beats it everywhere:

\[ \delta_{\mathrm{JS}}(x) = \Big(1-\frac{m-2}{|x|^2}\Big)x, \qquad R(\theta,\delta_{\mathrm{JS}}) = m-(m-2)^2\,E_\theta\frac{1}{|X|^2} \lt m . \]

It pulls every measurement toward the origin ("shrinks" it) by an amount read off from the data: when \(|X|^2\) is large compared with \(m\), the true vector is probably far from the origin and the pull is slight. The surprise is that measurements of unrelated quantities, star positions and batting averages, help each other through the common shrinkage factor. The effect is real: Efron and Morris's 1977 example predicted the end-of-season batting averages of eighteen players from their first forty-five at-bats, and the shrunken predictions beat the raw averages. Every modern shrinkage device, ridge regression, empirical Bayes, hierarchical models, image denoising, lives in this neighbourhood. The question of the paper is the natural next one: among all shrinkage rules, which ones cannot be beaten?

2. Priors, the posterior mean, and the shrinkage formula

A systematic way to produce shrinkage rules is to start from a prior: a measure \(F\) on \(\mathbb R^m\) that says where the true values are likely to be. Bayes' rule then gives the best guess as the average of \(\theta\) against the prior, reweighted by how well each \(\theta\) explains the measurement. With \(\varphi\) the standard normal density on \(\mathbb R^m\), the measurement \(x\) has likelihood \(\varphi(x-\theta)\) under \(\theta\), and the posterior mean is

\[ \delta_F(x) = \frac{\int \theta\,\varphi(x-\theta)\,F(d\theta)}{\int \varphi(x-\theta)\,F(d\theta)} . \]

The denominator is a function that will be with us for the rest of the page:

\[ f^*(x) = \int \varphi(x-\theta)\,F(d\theta) = (\varphi * F)(x), \]

the Gaussian blur of the prior, also called its marginal, because it is the density of the measurement \(X\) when \(\theta\) is itself drawn from \(F\). One line of calculus turns the posterior mean into a formula in \(f^*\) alone. Since \(\nabla_x\varphi(x-\theta)=-(x-\theta)\varphi(x-\theta)\),

\[ \nabla f^*(x) = \int (\theta-x)\,\varphi(x-\theta)\,F(d\theta) = f^*(x)\,\big(\delta_F(x)-x\big), \]

and therefore

The shrinkage formula (Tweedie; Brown)

\[ \delta_F(x) = x + \nabla \log f^*(x). \]

The estimate is the measurement, pushed in the direction in which the blurred prior increases: uphill on the landscape \(\log f^*\).

Three remarks make this family as large as it needs to be. First, \(F\) need not have finite total mass. The flat prior \(F=d\theta\) (Lebesgue measure) gives \(f^*\equiv1\) and \(\delta_F=X\); Stein's harmonic prior \(F=|\theta|^{-(m-2)}d\theta\) gives \(f^*\asymp|x|^{-(m-2)}\) for large \(|x|\) and a rule that behaves like James–Stein far from the origin. All that is needed is that the blur \(f^*(x)\) is finite at every \(x\). Such rules are called generalized Bayes estimators. Second, Brown (1971) proved that every admissible estimator of this problem is of this form. So nothing is lost by restricting to the family, and the whole question becomes: for which landscapes \(f^*\) is \(\delta_F\) admissible? Third, the formula shows that only \(f^*\) matters, not \(F\) itself. Two priors with the same blur give the same rule.

3. Gaussian blur, in pictures

The convolution \(\varphi*F\) replaces every unit of prior mass sitting at a point \(\theta\) by a Gaussian bump of unit variance centred at \(\theta\), and adds the bumps up. The same operation appears all over science:

  • Optics. A lens with a Gaussian point-spread function turns a sharp image \(F\) into the blurred photograph \(\varphi*F\).
  • Heat. If \(F\) is the initial temperature profile of a long bar, the temperature after one unit of time is \(\varphi*F\). Blur with variance \(t\) is the temperature at time \(t\): blurring and heat flow are the same thing.
  • Diffusion. A pattern of ink drops in still water spreads into \(\varphi_t*F\) at time \(t\).
  • Probability. The density of a sum of two independent random quantities is the convolution of their densities. That is why the blur appears here: \(X=\theta+\text{noise}\).

Two properties of the blur carry the whole paper. It is smoothing: \(f^*\) is infinitely differentiable even when \(F\) is a sum of spikes. And it is local at scale one: a unit Gaussian is negligible beyond a distance of four or five, so the blur cannot change the shape of a landscape at scales of ten, a hundred, a thousand. The large-scale geometry of the prior survives the blur, which is why one can design priors by drawing landscapes (Section 14).

Part II

Random motion and weighted spaces

4. Random walks and Brownian motion

Flip a coin every second and step left or right on a line; or step north, south, east or west on a grid. That is a random walk. Make the steps tiny and frequent, with step length \(h\) and time step \(h^2\), and let \(h\to0\): the jagged path converges to a continuous random curve, Brownian motion. The name is the botanist Robert Brown's, who in 1827 watched pollen grains jitter in water; Einstein explained the jitter in 1905 as the net effect of molecular collisions, Perrin measured it to count atoms, and Wiener built the mathematical object in 1923. The Brown of this page is Lawrence D. Brown, a statistician; the two are unrelated.

Three facts about Brownian motion are needed. Its displacement after time \(t\) is Gaussian with variance \(t\) in each coordinate, so a typical distance from the start is \(\sqrt t\): four times the time, twice the distance (the square-root law of diffusion). It has no memory: what it does after time \(t\) is a fresh Brownian motion started where it is. And its density spreads by the heat equation, which is the Gaussian blur of the previous section with variance \(t\).

The fact that matters most is Pólya's theorem (1921): a random walk on the line or the plane returns to its starting point with probability one, again and again, forever; in three or more dimensions it returns only with probability less than one, and eventually wanders off for good. The same holds for Brownian motion with "returns to the starting point" replaced by "returns to any small ball around it". The dichotomy has names: recurrent in dimensions one and two, transient from dimension three on. A remark attributed to Kakutani: a drunk man will find his way home, a drunk bird may be lost forever.

Here is the back-of-an-envelope reason, which will grow into the volume test of Section 12. At time \(t\) the walker is spread over a region of radius about \(\sqrt t\), which in \(d\) dimensions contains about \(t^{d/2}\) grid points. So the chance of being exactly at home at time \(t\) is about \(t^{-d/2}\), and the expected number of returns is \(\sum_t t^{-d/2}\): infinite exactly when \(d\le2\). An infinite expected number of returns means returning with probability one (if each return were uncertain, the expected count would be a finite geometric sum).

5. Three kinds of Brownian motion, and what they model

The paper uses Brownian motion in three guises. They are introduced here with the physical processes they describe, because the intuition for the theorems comes from those processes.

(a) Plain Brownian motion

A particle in a homogeneous medium, no forces. Its density \(u(t,x)\) obeys the heat equation \(\partial_t u=\Delta u\) (the factor in front of \(\Delta\) is a choice of time unit, and the paper's choice is one). Pollen in still water; heat in a uniform block; a stock's log-price in the simplest model.

(b) Brownian motion with a drift

A particle that is pushed by a force field \(b(x)\) while being jostled by noise: in the overdamped Langevin description, \(dZ_t=b(Z_t)\,dt+\sqrt2\,dW_t\). A grain in a flowing liquid (\(b\) is the flow velocity), a charged particle in an electric field, a bead in an optical trap. When the force comes from a potential, \(b=-\nabla V\), the particle's long-run occupation density is the Boltzmann weight \(e^{-V}\): it spends its time in the valleys of \(V\). The generator of the process, the operator that gives the rate of change of expectations, is

\[ L u = \Delta u + b\cdot\nabla u . \]

Now set \(e^{-V}=f^*\), that is, \(b=\nabla\log f^*\). This is exactly the drift in the shrinkage formula of Section 2, and the resulting process is Brown's diffusion: a thermal particle in the potential \(V=-\log f^*\), rolling down into the valleys of \(V\), which are the hills of \(f^*\). "Nudged uphill on \(f^*\)" and "falling downhill on \(V\)" are the same sentence.

(c) Brownian motion in an inhomogeneous medium: the weighted space

Take a positive weight \(w(x)\) and the operator

\[ L u = \frac{1}{w}\,\operatorname{div}(w\,\nabla u) = \Delta u + \nabla\log w\cdot\nabla u . \]

The right-hand side is (b) with \(b=\nabla\log w\), so this is the same process. The left-hand side is a different reading of it: heat flowing in a material whose conductivity and heat capacity are both \(w\); electric current in a conductor of conductivity \(w\); groundwater in rock of permeability \(w\) (Darcy's law). The natural quantity of this reading is the energy (or Dirichlet form)

\[ \mathcal E(u)=\int|\nabla u|^2\,w\,dx , \]

the power dissipated by the voltage pattern \(u\) in the conductor \(w\). The process (b)/(c) with \(w=f^*\) is called the weighted Brownian motion of the weighted space \((\mathbb R^m,f^*dx)\), and that phrase is the one the paper uses.

(d) Brownian motion on a curved space

On a Riemannian manifold, a surface or higher-dimensional space with its own notion of distance, the generator is the Laplace–Beltrami operator and the particle diffuses along the curved geometry. This guise is needed only in Section 20, where the space of probability distributions of a statistical model turns out to carry a natural curved geometry.

6. Weighted spaces: ordinary distances, volume counted with a weight

The weighted space \((\mathbb R^m, w\,dx)\) is ordinary space with ordinary distances, in which the volume of a region is not its Euclidean volume but its mass under the weight:

\[ \mu(A) = \int_A w(x)\,dx . \]

A map on which population density varies is a weighted plane: the "volume" of a disc is the number of people in it. A block of porous rock is a weighted solid: the volume of a region is its pore space. The particle of Section 5(c) treats \(\mu\) as its natural count of places; the heavier a region, the more time it spends there, and the easier it is for current to pass. The paper writes

  • \(B(x,\rho)\) for the ball of radius \(\rho\) about \(x\), and \(B_r=B(0,r)\);
  • \(V(r)=\mu(B_r)\) for the mass of the ball of radius \(r\) about the origin;
  • \(\bar f(r)\) for the average of \(w\) over the sphere \(\{|x|=r\}\), so that \(V'(r)=\omega_{m-1}r^{m-1}\bar f(r)\): the mass of a thin shell is its area times its average weight times its thickness.

One convention is used constantly. Two positive quantities are comparable, written \(a\asymp b\), if their ratio stays between two fixed positive constants. Two weights that are comparable produce comparable energies, comparable ball masses, comparable sphere averages. Everything in this story (recurrence, the two tests, doubling, Poincaré) is insensitive to constants and depends only on the shape of the weight at large scales. So "\(w\asymp w'\)" will mean "the same for all our purposes", and the comparison lemma of Section 14 will say that the Gaussian blur of a reasonable weight is comparable to the weight itself.

7. Coming home, in electrical language

The weighted Brownian motion is recurrent if, with probability one, it returns to every ball at arbitrarily late times, and transient if it eventually leaves every ball for good. Deciding which is hard to do by watching paths, and the useful tool is an electrical one, in the spirit of Doyle and Snell's monograph Random walks and electric networks. A random walk on a graph corresponds to a resistor network in which the conductance of an edge is the rate at which the walk crosses it; in the continuum the conductance becomes the conductivity \(w\) of Section 5(c).

Connect the unit ball \(B_1\) to a battery and "ground at infinity". A voltage \(u\) with \(u=1\) on the ball and \(u=0\) far away dissipates the power \(\mathcal E(u)=\int|\nabla u|^2w\,dx\). The smallest possible dissipation is the capacity of the ball,

\[ \operatorname{cap}(B_1) = \inf\Big\{\int|\nabla u|^2\,w\,dx:\ u=1\text{ on }B_1,\ u \text{ vanishes outside some bounded set}\Big\}, \]

which is the effective conductance from the ball to infinity; its reciprocal is the effective resistance \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}\). Two variational principles say that nature finds this minimum: the Dirichlet principle (the true voltage minimises the dissipation among voltages) and the Thomson principle (the true current minimises it among unit currents). The connection with the particle is the fundamental theorem of the subject:

Recurrence = infinite resistance to infinity

The weighted Brownian motion of \((\mathbb R^m,w\,dx)\) is recurrent if and only if \(\operatorname{cap}(B_1)=0\), that is, if and only if the resistance from a ball to infinity is infinite. (Brown 1971, Theorem 4.3.1; Grigor'yan 1999, Theorem 5.1.)

Here is the physics behind it. Take a metal sphere of radius \(a\) buried in an infinite medium of conductivity \(\sigma\), the earth. The potential around it is \(a/r\), the current it sends to infinity under unit voltage is \(4\pi\sigma a\), and the resistance to infinity is \(1/(4\pi\sigma a)\): finite. That is why an earthing electrode works, and it is the electrical face of Pólya's theorem: three-dimensional space is transient, current escapes. Now take a disc of radius \(a\) in a thin conducting sheet. The potential is \(\log(R/r)/\log(R/a)\) out to a circle of radius \(R\), the resistance is \(\log(R/a)/(2\pi\sigma)\), and it grows without bound as \(R\to\infty\): a plane is recurrent, no current escapes. The heat version: a sphere held at a fixed temperature in an infinite three-dimensional block settles into a steady state with a finite heat loss; a disc in an infinite plate never does.

The subject has accumulated synonyms, and the paper uses several. They all mean the same thing:

recurrent (probability)parabolic (geometry)capacity zero (potential theory)infinite resistance (electricity)no Green function (analysis)
the particle returns to every ball foreverthe space is of "parabolic type", like the plane in the classification of surfaces\(\operatorname{cap}(B_1)=0\)\(R_{\mathrm{eff}}(B_1\to\infty)=\infty\)the expected total time spent in a ball is infinite

The opposite column reads transient, non-parabolic, positive capacity, finite resistance, a Green function exists. The paper's title word parabolicity is simply recurrence under its geometric name.

Part III

Brown's theorem and his two tests

8. The dictionary: admissible means "the particle comes home"

Everything is now in place to state the 1971 theorem that the paper builds on. Attach to the prior \(F\) its blur \(f^*\) and the weighted Brownian motion of \((\mathbb R^m,f^*dx)\), the thermal particle of Section 5 that rolls into the hills of \(f^*\).

Theorem (Brown 1971)

(a) (Theorem 4.3.1) The particle is transient if and only if the unit ball has positive capacity in \((\mathbb R^m,f^*dx)\).

(b) (Theorem 5.1.1) If the particle is transient, \(\delta_F\) is inadmissible. If the particle is recurrent and the push \(\nabla\log f^*\) is bounded, \(\delta_F\) is admissible.

Part (a) is the electrical dictionary of Section 7, proved by Brown for this setting before the geometric literature had it in general. Part (b) is the bridge to statistics. Here is the idea behind it, with constants suppressed. Take a function \(h\) equal to \(1\) on the unit ball and vanishing outside some large ball, and consider the proper prior \(h^2F\). Its Bayes rule differs from \(\delta_F\) by \(\nabla\log h^2\), and an integration by parts (Stein's identity) shows that the Bayes-risk gap between the two, averaged against \(h^2F\), is proportional to the energy \(\int|\nabla h|^2f^*dx\) of \(h\) in the conductor \(f^*\). If the capacity is zero, that energy can be made as small as we please while \(h=1\) on any fixed ball: \(\delta_F\) is then as good as a proper Bayes rule on every bounded region, and a classical argument of Blyth shows that no competitor can beat it. If the capacity is positive, the equilibrium voltage itself, the minimiser in the capacity, yields a competitor that does beat \(\delta_F\). The bounded-drift condition is technical and is satisfied by every prior in the paper.

The theorem explains Stein's threshold. The flat prior gives \(f^*\equiv1\), the estimator \(X\), and plain Brownian motion: recurrent for \(m\le2\), transient for \(m\ge3\). So \(X\) is admissible in dimensions one and two and inadmissible from three on, and the reason is Pólya's theorem, or, if you prefer, the earthing electrode. Stein's harmonic prior has \(f^*\asymp|x|^{-(m-2)}\), which is exactly the potential of a point charge: the weighted space it defines is on the recurrent side (Section 9 checks this with the sphere test), so the generalized Bayes rule of the harmonic prior is admissible. The James–Stein rule of Section 1 is itself inadmissible, as its positive-part version beats it, but the admissible rule from the harmonic prior behaves like it at large \(|x|\).

9. The two radial tests, and the zone they leave open

Capacity is hard to compute, so Brown gave two tests that use only radial information: averages of \(f^*\) over spheres, and the values of \(f^*\) along rays. Both are resistor arguments.

Sphere test for recurrence (Brown 1971, Theorem 6.4.3)

If \( \displaystyle\int_1^\infty \frac{dr}{r^{m-1}\bar f(r)} = \infty \), the particle is recurrent, and \(\delta_F\) is admissible if the drift is bounded.

Why. Current flowing from the ball to infinity must cross every sphere. A thin spherical shell of radius \(r\), thickness \(dr\) and average conductivity \(\bar f(r)\) has resistance \(dr/(\omega_{m-1}r^{m-1}\bar f(r))\), and shells are in series: resistances add. If the sum diverges, the resistance to infinity is infinite.

Ray test for transience (Brown 1971, Theorem 6.4.4)

If there is a set \(\Omega\) of directions of positive solid angle such that the ray integrals \( \displaystyle\int_1^\infty \frac{dr}{r^{m-1}f^*(r\varphi)} \) are bounded uniformly for \(\varphi\in\Omega\), the particle is transient and \(\delta_F\) is inadmissible.

Why. A thin cone of rays is a bundle of one-dimensional conductors in parallel. If each ray has finite resistance to infinity and the bundle has positive cross-section, their parallel combination has finite resistance, and current escapes along it.

A worked example shows the tests in action. For a radial weight \(w=(1+|x|)^{-\beta}\) the integrand of both tests is \(r^{\beta-m+1}\) up to constants, which has a divergent integral exactly when \(\beta\ge m-2\). So the sphere test fires for \(\beta\ge m-2\), the ray test fires for \(\beta\lt m-2\), and between them they decide every radial weight; Brown's Theorem 6.3.1 says exactly this for spherically symmetric priors. The harmonic prior is the borderline case \(\beta=m-2\), recurrent. Priors that decay faster than the point-charge potential give inadmissible rules; priors that decay no faster give admissible ones.

For non-radial weights the two tests can both fall silent, as Brown noted on page 900 of his paper. The sphere test is silent when \(\int dr/(r^{m-1}\bar f)\) converges: the spheres are, on average, good conductors. The ray test is silent when no cone of positive solid angle has bounded ray integrals: essentially every single ray is a poor conductor. Both at once means spheres that are heavy on average but crossed by valleys that every ray must traverse. The paper calls the set of such priors Brown's undecided zone, and its subject is what decides admissibility inside it.

Two small results in the paper locate the zone. The spherical \(A_2\) ratio \(Q(r)=\bar f(r)\cdot A(r)\), where \(A(r)\) is the sphere average of \(1/w\), is at least \(1\) and measures how unevenly the weight is spread over the sphere of radius \(r\). If \(Q\) stays bounded, a Chebyshev argument shows that one of Brown's tests always fires (Lemma 3.2): every weight in the zone has \(Q(r)\to\infty\) along some sequence of radii, so its spheres must contain both heavy and very light parts. And if the valleys come from one coordinate only, \(w(x)=v(x_1)\) with \(v\) doubling and Poincaré in the sense of Part IV, then \(Q\) is bounded and \(\delta_F\) is inadmissible for \(m\ge3\) (Lemma 3.3): a valley made by a single coordinate is a wall across space, and a wall is too blunt an object to enter the zone. Valleys that enter the zone and stay regular must be thinner than walls, and in \(\mathbb R^3\) the next thinner thing is a curve (Theorem 3.4). That observation drives the construction of Part V.

Part IV

The geometric toolbox

Since the 1980s, recurrence on weighted spaces and manifolds has its own theory (Lyons and Sullivan, Varopoulos, Grigor'yan, Saloff-Coste, Sturm). Its tests are not radial, and they come with two regularity conditions on the weight that make the tests sharp. Both conditions have a physical meaning.

10. Doubling: no scale carries a sudden concentration of mass

The measure \(\mu=w\,dx\) is doubling if there is a constant \(D\) with

\[ \mu(B(x,2\rho)) \le D\,\mu(B(x,\rho)) \qquad\text{for every centre } x \text{ and every radius } \rho . \]

Doubling the radius of any ball multiplies its mass by at most a fixed factor. Lebesgue measure is doubling with \(D=2^m\); the power weights \(|x|^{a}\) with \(a\gt-m\) are doubling; a weight that grows like \(e^{|x|}\) is not, because the outer half of a large ball carries almost everything; and a weight equal to \(1\) inside a slab and to \(10^{-6}\) outside it is not either, because a ball that just reaches the slab has a tiny mass while its double has a large one. Doubling implies polynomial growth, \(V(r)\le Cr^{\log_2 D}\), and a mild converse called reverse doubling. In the population-density picture it says that no town is a point: zooming out from any place at any scale, the population grows gradually.

11. Poincaré: no walls, and the slowest mode of a drum

The second condition is the scale-invariant Poincaré inequality: there is a constant \(C_P\) with

\[ \int_B |u-u_B|^2\,d\mu \;\le\; C_P\,\rho^2 \int_B |\nabla u|^2\,d\mu \qquad\text{for every ball } B \text{ of radius } \rho \text{ and every smooth } u, \]

where \(u_B\) is the \(\mu\)-average of \(u\) on \(B\). The left side is the variance of \(u\) over the ball, counted with the weight; the right side is its energy. The inequality says that a function cannot vary a lot across a ball without paying for it in energy, at the exchange rate \(\rho^2\). What breaks it is a wall: suppose a thin sheet of very small weight cuts the ball in two heavy halves. Take \(u=+1\) on one side and \(-1\) on the other, with the transition inside the sheet. The variance is about \(\mu(B)\), but all the gradient sits where \(w\approx0\), so the energy is nearly zero. The ratio of the two sides is huge, and it gets worse as the ball grows. So the Poincaré inequality is the statement no walls at any scale.

There is a sharper way to say this. The best constant on a given ball is \(1/(\lambda_1\rho^2)\), where \(\lambda_1\) is the first nonzero eigenvalue of the weighted Laplacian \(-w^{-1}\operatorname{div}(w\nabla\,\cdot)\) on \(B\) with Neumann (insulated) boundary condition. Physically, \(\lambda_1\) is the slowest relaxation rate of heat in the ball: insulate the ball's surface, start from any temperature pattern, and the pattern decays to uniform, its slowest component as \(e^{-\lambda_1t}\). For a uniform ball \(\lambda_1\) is a constant divided by \(\rho^2\), the familiar law that a potato twice as large takes four times as long to cook, so \(\lambda_1\rho^2\) is a pure number: \(\pi^2/4=2.47\) for a cube of half-side \(\rho\), and \(4.33\) for the axially symmetric modes of a ball. A ball cut by a wall is a dumbbell, two rooms joined by a narrow door; its slowest mode is "warm room, cold room", which relaxes only through the door, and \(\lambda_1\rho^2\) is tiny. The scale-invariant Poincaré inequality is the statement that \(\lambda_1\rho^2\) stays bounded below over all balls of all sizes. This is also how the inequality is checked numerically in Part VI.

12. The heat kernel and the volume test

Write \(p_t(x,y)\) for the density, with respect to \(\mu\), of the particle's position at time \(t\) when it starts at \(x\): the heat kernel. For plain Brownian motion it is the Gaussian bump of variance \(2t\). For a weighted space that is doubling and Poincaré, Saloff-Coste (1992) and Grigor'yan (1991) proved that the heat kernel is still Gaussian up to constants:

\[ \frac{c}{V(x,\sqrt t)}\,e^{-C|x-y|^2/t} \;\le\; p_t(x,y)\;\le\; \frac{C}{V(x,\sqrt t)}\,e^{-c|x-y|^2/t}, \qquad V(x,\rho)=\mu(B(x,\rho)). \]

In words: at time \(t\) the particle is spread, roughly evenly in the \(\mu\)-sense, over the ball of radius \(\sqrt t\) around its start. In particular the return density is \(p_t(x,x)\asymp1/V(x,\sqrt t)\). Now recall from Section 4 that recurrence means an infinite expected number of returns, which in continuous time is \(\int_1^\infty p_t(x,x)\,dt=\infty\). Substituting the bound and changing variables \(t=r^2\):

Volume test (Karp, Varopoulos, Grigor'yan; an equivalence under doubling + Poincaré)

\[ \int_1^\infty \frac{r\,dr}{V(r)} = \infty \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{recurrent, for every weight (Grigor'yan 1999, Theorem 7.3);} \]

and if the weight is doubling and Poincaré, the converse holds too, so recurrence is equivalent to the divergence of the integral (Grigor'yan 1999, Theorem 11.1, with the heat-kernel bounds of Saloff-Coste 1992, Theorems 3.1 and 4.2).

Pólya's theorem is the case \(V(r)\propto r^d\): the integrand is \(r^{1-d}\), divergent exactly for \(d\le2\). The volume test is attractive because it asks only how much mass the balls carry, not how it is arranged. It is also weaker than Brown's sphere test for a general weight (a Cauchy–Schwarz exercise, Section 15 below), and the point of the doubling + Poincaré hypothesis is that it makes the weak test exact. Section 17 shows a weight for which the volume test gives the wrong answer because Poincaré fails: the hypothesis is not decorative.

13. The flux bound: resistors in series along any family of surfaces

Brown's sphere test adds up spherical shells in series. Nothing forces the shells to be spherical. Take any family of nested closed surfaces \(S^{(s)}\), each enclosing the unit ball, and the thin layers between consecutive ones. A unit current from the ball to infinity must cross every surface, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality shows that the dissipation inside a layer is at least \(1/(\text{conductance of the layer})\), where the conductance is the weighted area \(\int_{S}w\,dA\) divided by the thickness. Layers that do not overlap add up (this is the Nash-Williams inequality for networks; for the continuum see Grigor'yan 1999, Theorem 7.6, and Lemma 5.1 of the paper):

\[ R_{\mathrm{eff}}(B_1\to\infty)\;\ge\;\sum_{\text{layers}}\frac{\text{thickness}}{\int_S w\,dA}. \]

If the sum is infinite, the capacity is zero and the space is recurrent. The freedom to choose the surfaces is the whole point: a weight can have cheap spheres yet expensive surfaces of another shape, and the right exhaustion finds them. This is the tool that decides the wall example of Section 17, where the surfaces follow the wall.

Part V

The paper's results

With the vocabulary in place, the paper's contribution can be stated in four pieces: a lemma that lets one draw priors as landscapes; Theorem A, which says that inside the doubling + Poincaré class Brown's sphere test was already complete; Theorem B, an explicit prior in the undecided zone that the volume test decides; and the examples that show the zone is two-sided outside that class.

14. The comparison lemma: landscapes are priors

Brown's theorem speaks about Gaussian blurs \(f^*=\varphi*F\). The weights the paper constructs are written down directly, as functions of the distance to a set of curves. The following lemma says that this is allowed.

Lemma 2.3 (comparison)

Let \(w\gt0\) on \(\mathbb R^m\) be locally integrable with \(\log w\) Lipschitz with constant \(L\), and let \(f^*=\varphi*w\) be the marginal of the prior \(F=w\,d\theta\). Then

(i) \(c_L\,w\le f^*\le C_L\,w\) with \(c_L=E\,e^{-L|Z|}\gt0\) and \(C_L=E\,e^{L|Z|}\lt\infty\), where \(Z\sim N(0,I_m)\);
(ii) \(|\nabla\log f^*|\le L\), so the drift is bounded;
(iii) \(F\) is \(\sigma\)-finite and \(f^*\lt\infty\), so Brown's theorem applies to \(\delta_F\);
(iv) the spaces \((\mathbb R^m,w\,dx)\) and \((\mathbb R^m,f^*dx)\) have comparable energies, hence the same recurrence type; their doubling constants, Poincaré constants, sphere means, ray integrals and ball masses are comparable; Brown's tests are silent for \(w\) exactly when they are silent for \(f^*\), and \(w\) is doubling + Poincaré exactly when \(f^*\) is.

Why it is true. "\(\log w\) is Lipschitz with constant \(L\)" means that moving a distance \(|z|\) changes \(w\) by a factor between \(e^{-L|z|}\) and \(e^{L|z|}\). The blur at \(x\) averages \(w(x+z)\) over Gaussian \(z\), so it lies between \(w(x)\,E e^{-L|Z|}\) and \(w(x)\,E e^{L|Z|}\); that is (i). The gradient of the blur is the blur of \(w\nabla\log w\), whose size is at most \(L\) times the blur of \(w\); that is (ii). And (iv) is (i) applied inside every integral in sight. For \(m=3\) and \(L=2\), the constants are \(c_L=0.085\) and \(C_L=73.8\): large, but constants never matter here (Section 6).

The lemma is small, but it changes how one works. Instead of guessing a prior and computing its blur, one draws the landscape \(w\) one wants, checks that its logarithm has bounded slope, and knows that \(F=w\,d\theta\) is a genuine prior whose blur has the same recurrence type, the same test verdicts and the same regularity. Some weights are blurs exactly: for a line \(\ell\) in \(\mathbb R^3\), the blur of \(\operatorname{dist}(\theta,\ell)^4\) is \(\operatorname{dist}(x,\ell)^4+8\operatorname{dist}(x,\ell)^2+8\), comparable to \((1+\operatorname{dist}(x,\ell)^2)^2\).

15. Theorem A: inside the doubling + Poincaré class, Brown's sphere test was already complete

Theorem A (Theorem 3.1 of the paper)

Let \(w\) be doubling and Poincaré on \(\mathbb R^m\), \(m\ge2\). The following are equivalent:

(i) \((\mathbb R^m,w\,dx)\) is parabolic (the particle is recurrent);
(ii) \(\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dr}{r^{m-1}\bar f(r)}=\infty\) (Brown's sphere test fires);
(iii) \(\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{r\,dr}{V(r)}=\infty\) (the volume test fires).

Consequently, if \(f^*\) is doubling and Poincaré with bounded drift, \(\delta_F\) is admissible if and only if Brown's sphere test fires. The ray test implies transience but is not necessary for it: the weight of Theorem B is doubling and Poincaré and transient with the ray test silent.

The Cauchy–Schwarz step: why the volume test can never fire without the sphere test

Write \(V'(r)=\omega_{m-1}r^{m-1}\bar f(r)\), so that the sphere-test integrand is \(1/V'\) up to a constant. On any interval \([R,2R]\), Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[ R^2=\Big(\int_R^{2R}dr\Big)^2 \le \int_R^{2R}V'\,dr\cdot\int_R^{2R}\frac{dr}{V'} \le V(2R)\int_R^{2R}\frac{dr}{V'} , \]

while the volume-test integrand satisfies \(\int_R^{2R}r\,dr/V\le2R^2/V(R)\). Summing over \(R=2^j\): if the volume integral diverges, then \(\sum_j4^j/V(2^{j+1})\) diverges, and the first display shows that \(\int dr/V'\) diverges too. So "volume test fires" implies "sphere test fires", for every weight, with no hypothesis at all. The converse needs doubling + Poincaré, and that is what Theorem A adds.

What the theorem says in practice. If your prior is regular in the sense of Part IV, one integral decides admissibility, and it is the integral Brown wrote down in 1971. The modern geometric criteria cannot find a new admissible case in this class, because every admissible case is already detected by the sphere test. They can only find new inadmissible cases, transient weights that the ray test misses, and Theorem B shows that such weights exist. That is the division of labour the paper establishes: on the admissible side, Brown was complete; on the inadmissible side, geometry adds something.

Three further statements in Section 3 of the paper say where in the zone such weights must live, and were quoted in Section 9: Lemma 3.2 (bounded spherical \(A_2\) ratio makes Brown's tests exhaustive), Lemma 3.3 (one-dimensional doubling + Poincaré forces a bounded \(A_2\) ratio, so product weights \(v(x_1)\) never enter the zone while staying regular, and give inadmissible rules for \(m\ge3\)), and Theorem 3.4, which is the first positive sign:

Theorem 3.4 (co-dimension-two valleys)

For \(m\ge3\) and \(\alpha\ge0\) the weight \(w(x)=(1+x_1^2+\dots+x_{m-1}^2)^{\alpha/2}\), a power of the distance to the \(x_m\)-axis, is doubling and Poincaré; and for \(\alpha\gt m-1\) its spherical \(A_2\) ratio grows like \(r^{\alpha-(m-1)}\to\infty\).

The doubling + Poincaré part is a theorem of J. Björn (2001, Proposition 6), which handles powers of the distance to a subspace of co-dimension at least two, combined with a self-improvement result of Hajłasz and Koskela (2000, Theorems 5.1 and 9.7) that upgrades the weak form of the inequality to the form used here. The growth of the \(A_2\) ratio says that a valley along a line is unevenly spread over every large sphere, which is the entry ticket to the zone that Lemma 3.2 demands. A single line does not silence the ray test, since most rays never come near it; the construction of the next section makes valleys sweep every direction.

16. Theorem B: a prior that neither test decides, built to order

The task is to build a weight on \(\mathbb R^3\) that is doubling and Poincaré, on which both of Brown's tests are silent, and whose ball mass grows like \(r^3\), so that the volume test says transient and \(\delta_F\) is inadmissible. Three requirements push against each other:

  • to silence the ray test, every direction must meet a low-conductivity valley in every dyadic shell \(2^k\le|x|\lt2^{k+1}\), so that each shell costs every ray a fixed amount;
  • to keep the sphere test silent and the volume \(\asymp r^3\), the valleys must occupy a vanishing fraction of each shell;
  • to keep Poincaré, the valleys must never join up into a wall.

Curves do all three. A circle in \(\mathbb R^3\) is a one-dimensional set, of co-dimension two: a thin tube of low weight around it does not cut space, so it is not a wall (Section 9, right-hand sphere). The paper places, in each shell \(k\), a family of \(N_k=2^{k+2}\) horizontal circles (circles of latitude about the \(z\)-axis) at radii between \(2^k\) and \(2^{k+1}\), arranged so that their polar angles run through all the values \(\pi(n+\tfrac12)/N_k\), \(n=0,\dots,N_k-1\). Then every direction from the origin is within an angle \(\pi/(2N_k)\) of some circle in shell \(k\), which at radius \(2^{k+1}\) is a distance of at most \(\pi/4\). Seen in a plane through the axis (a meridian half-plane) the circles are points, and they form a slanted rectangular lattice with spacing about \(s_k=2^{k/2}\) in both directions. The weight is

\[ w(x)=\Big(\frac{1+d(x)^2}{1+|x|+d(x)^2}\Big)^{\alpha/2}, \qquad d(x)=\operatorname{dist}(x,\text{all the circles}),\quad \alpha\ge4 . \]

Near a circle at radius \(r\) this is \((1+d^2)^{\alpha/2}\) divided by \(r^{\alpha/2}\): the valley of Theorem 3.4 around the circle, normalised so that the weight climbs back to about \(1\) at distance \(\sqrt r\), which is the spacing \(s_k\). Away from all circles (\(d\ge s_k\)) the weight is at least \(2^{-\alpha/2}\). So the set where \(w\) is small is a union of thin tubes around circles that are \(s_k\) apart, and no surface of small weight exists at any scale. Two numbers are forced by arithmetic:

  • The spacing \(s_k=2^{k/2}\). Shell \(k\) has volume about \(8^k\) and must host \(N_k\asymp2^k\) circles of length about \(2^k\) at a common mutual distance \(s\). Each circle commands a tube of volume about \(2^k s^2\), so \(2^k\cdot2^ks^2\asymp8^k\), that is, \(s^2\asymp2^k\).
  • The exponent \(\alpha\ge4\). A ray that passes within \(\pi/4\) of a circle at radius \(r_0\) sees \(w\le(2.62/r_0)^{\alpha/2}\) on a segment of length \(2\), and pays at least \(0.28\,r_0^{\alpha/2-2}\) of ray integral there. At \(\alpha=4\) that is the constant \(0.28\) per shell, enough to make every ray integral diverge; for smaller \(\alpha\) the payments would decay geometrically and sum to a finite amount.
Theorem B (Theorem 4.1 of the paper)

Let \(w\) be the lattice weight above on \(\mathbb R^3\), \(\alpha\ge4\), and \(F=w\,d\theta\). Then (a) \(w\,dx\) is doubling and supports the scale-invariant Poincaré inequality on all balls; (b) Brown's sphere test is silent, with \(\bar f(r)\ge c\gt0\) for \(r\ge16\), and Brown's ray test is silent, with in fact \(\int_{16}^\infty dr/(r^2w(r\varphi))=\infty\) for every direction \(\varphi\); (c) \(V(r)\asymp r^3\). Consequently \((\mathbb R^3,f^*dx)\) is transient and \(\delta_F\) is inadmissible, although neither of Brown's radial tests applies.

Parts (b) and (c) are the arithmetic just described: every ray pays \(0.28\) per shell (that is (b) for the ray test); on every sphere a fixed fraction of the area lies at distance \(\ge s_k/4\) from all circles, where \(w\ge17^{-\alpha/2}\), so the sphere means are bounded below and the sphere integral converges (that is (b) for the sphere test), and the same fraction argument gives \(V(r)\ge cr^3\) while \(w\le1\) gives \(V(r)\le\frac43\pi r^3\). The consequence is then the volume test of Section 12: \(\int r\,dr/r^3\lt\infty\), transient under (a), and Brown's theorem (b) turns transience into inadmissibility, with the comparison lemma carrying everything from \(w\) to the genuine blur \(f^*\).

Part (a) is the technical centre of the paper. It is assembled from two lemmas:

Lemma 4.3 (one circle) and Lemma 4.2 (gluing)

One circle. For a circle \(C_R\subset\mathbb R^3\) of radius \(R\) and the weight \(w_R=(1+\operatorname{dist}(x,C_R)^2)^{\alpha/2}\), the measure \(w_R\,dx\) is doubling and Poincaré with constants independent of \(R\). (At scales below \(R/2\) a circle looks like a line, and Theorem 3.4 applies after a bi-Lipschitz change of coordinates; at scales above \(R/4\) the weight is comparable to the power weight \((1+|x-c|^2)^{\alpha/2}\) about the circle's centre, which is Björn's theorem again.)

Gluing. If a large ball is covered by cells of comparable size, each cell carries comparable mass under \(\mu\) and under a reference measure \(\nu\) that is doubling + Poincaré, and \(\mu\) satisfies the Poincaré inequality on each cell's neighbourhood, then \(\mu\) satisfies it on the large ball. (Proof: replace \(u\) by its cell averages, compare the result with \(u\) cell by cell, and control the cell averages through \(\nu\).)

Applied to the lattice: a ball of radius at most a quarter of the local spacing meets at most one circle and is handled by the one-circle lemma; a larger ball is covered by cells of side \(s/8\), each containing at most one circle; the cells have mass comparable to their Euclidean volume because the tube around a circle occupies a small fraction of its cell; and the gluing lemma, with \(\nu\) the Lebesgue measure, gives the inequality on the large ball. The pattern repeats from shell to shell up to a distortion of order \(1/s\), which is why the constants are uniform.

17. The zone is two-sided, and the wall explains why

What if the same circles are placed coherently, one per unit of radius, \(\Gamma_{k,j}=\{|x|=2^k+j,\ \theta=\pi(j+\tfrac12)/2^k\}\) for \(j\lt2^k\), with the weight \(w'=\big((1+d'^2)/(1+|x|^2)\big)^{\alpha/2}\), \(\alpha\ge2\)? Every ray still meets a tube in every shell, so the ray test is silent; the sphere means are still bounded below, so the sphere test is silent; the ball mass still grows like \(r^3\). One might expect transience. The opposite is true.

Proposition 5.2 (the ring weight is recurrent)

For \(\alpha\ge2\), \((\mathbb R^3,w'dx)\) is parabolic, and \(\delta_F\) is admissible for \(F=w'd\theta\). The weight is doubling but does not support the scale-invariant Poincaré inequality.

The circles of shell \(k\) lie on one surface of revolution \(\Sigma_k\), a closed cap running from radius \(2^k\) at the north pole to \(2^{k+1}\) at the south pole, and consecutive circles on it are at most \(6.4\) apart, so every point of \(\Sigma_k\) is within \(3.2\) of a circle. Above that mesh scale \(\Sigma_k\) is a wall of weight at most \((19/4^k)^{\alpha/2}\). The flux bound of Section 13 along the layers around the \(\Sigma_k\) gives a per-shell contribution of at least \(c\,4^{k(\alpha/2-1)}\) to the resistance: a constant for \(\alpha=2\) (numerically \(0.047\) per shell, flat), geometrically growing for larger \(\alpha\). The sum diverges, the capacity is zero, the particle is recurrent. And Poincaré fails exactly as Section 11 predicts: the two-room test function across \(\Sigma_k\) has a variance-to-energy ratio growing like \(\rho^{\alpha-1}\), which is the collapse in Figure 1(b), where the eigenvalue halves with every doubling of the ball for \(\alpha=2\). Theorem A had said in advance that this weight could not be doubling + Poincaré, since it is recurrent with the sphere test silent. The volume test, applied blindly, would have given the wrong answer; this is the example that shows the Poincaré hypothesis is essential.

A third member of the zone comes from one dimension, and it is transient:

Proposition 5.3 (the logarithmic product prior)

Let \(F(d\theta)=v(\theta_1)\,d\theta_1d\theta_2d\theta_3\) on \(\mathbb R^3\) with \(v(t)=(1+|t|)^{-1}\log(e+|t|)\). Then both of Brown's tests are silent, \(v\) is not doubling, and \((\mathbb R^3,f^*dx)\) is transient, so \(\delta_F\) is inadmissible.

The sphere mean is \(\bar f(r)\asymp\log^2r/r\), so the sphere integral converges; along every non-equatorial direction the ray integral is a multiple of \(\int dr/(r\log r)=\infty\), so the ray test is silent; and the doubling ratio grows like \(\log r\). Transience comes from the product structure: the weighted Brownian motion is a one-dimensional diffusion in the weight \(v\) times a plain two-dimensional Brownian motion, and such a product is transient exactly when \(\int^\infty p^W_t(0,0)\,t^{-1}dt\lt\infty\) for the one-dimensional return density \(p^W\). A Tauberian theorem of Kasahara (1975, Theorem 2(iii)) for one-dimensional diffusions with a slowly varying speed measure gives \(p^W_t(0,0)\sim4/\log^2t\), and \(\int dt/(t\log^2t)\) converges. The numerical check of Part VI watches \(\log^2t\cdot p^W_t(0,0)\) climb from \(2.58\) at \(t=10^3\) to \(3.44\) at \(t=10^7\), toward the limit \(4\). This is an instance of a phenomenon found by Johnstone and Lalley (1984): a one-dimensional generalized Bayes rule that is "just barely" admissible (null-recurrent) always has a product partner for which the product rule is inadmissible.

Part VI

The numbers, and what the balls mean

19. Reading the numerical section of the paper

The proofs in the paper are complete without computation. The numbers were used to find the constructions and to check the uniformity claims that a proof sketch can hide: that the Poincaré constant of the lattice really does not drift with the scale, that every ray really pays its toll, that the wall really collapses as predicted. This section explains what was computed, what the "balls" are, and how to read the table.

What is being measured

For a ball \(B=B(x_0,\rho)\) the best Poincaré constant is \(1/(\lambda_1\rho^2)\), with \(\lambda_1\) the first nonzero eigenvalue of \(-w^{-1}\operatorname{div}(w\nabla\,\cdot)\) on \(B\) with insulated boundary (Section 11). Physically: fill the ball with a material of conductivity and heat capacity \(w\), insulate its surface, and ask how fast an initial temperature pattern evens out. The slowest pattern decays like \(e^{-\lambda_1t}\); its time scale is \(1/\lambda_1\), the mixing time of the ball. Because diffusion times grow with the square of the size, \(\lambda_1\rho^2\) is the number to compare across scales: a constant for the uniform ball, and collapsing toward zero if the ball contains a wall that splits it into rooms. The scale-invariant Poincaré inequality is the statement that \(\lambda_1\rho^2\) stays bounded below for every ball in the space. One can only compute finitely many balls, so one chooses the ones that could fail: balls at the scale of the spacing, where a single tube is a large feature of the ball; balls at many spacings, where a lattice of tubes could conspire; balls in different shells, where the spacing changes; and balls centred on a tube, in the bulk, and on the axis of symmetry.

How it is computed

The ball is cut into small cubic cells. Each cell gets a mass equal to the weight times the cell volume, and each face between two neighbouring cells gets a conductance equal to the geometric mean of the two weights, divided by the face spacing. The result is a resistor network with capacitors at the nodes, which is the discrete version of the heat equation in the weighted medium; its relaxation rates are the eigenvalues of a generalized eigenproblem \(Ku=\lambda Mu\), solved by a shift-invert Lanczos iteration for the smallest few. The Lebesgue controls, \(\pi^2/4=2.467\) for a square and \(4.333\) for a ball's axisymmetric modes, are reproduced to three digits, and every tabulated value is unchanged to the quoted digits under refinement of the mesh. For balls centred on the symmetry axis of the construction the problem reduces to two dimensions: the modes that vary with the angle around the axis satisfy the Poincaré inequality automatically with constant \(1\) (their gradient around the axis already controls them), so only the axisymmetric modes need computing, on a half-disc with the measure \(2\pi s\,w\,ds\,dz\); this reaches balls of radius \(256\) at mesh \(0.5\). Off the axis there is no reduction, and the paper runs a full three-dimensional computation on cubes with up to \(2.6\cdot10^5\) cells.

Part VII

The parameter side, uses, open questions, glossary and sources

20. The same dichotomy on the parameter side

Brown's particle lives on the space of measurements. There is a second place in statistics where "does the particle come home?" decides a question, and it lives on the space of parameters. Three ideas are needed.

The Fisher metric. For a statistical model \(\{p(x\mid\theta)\}\), the Fisher information \(g_{ij}(\theta)=E_\theta[\partial_i\log p\,\partial_j\log p]\) measures how distinguishable the distributions at \(\theta\) and at \(\theta+d\theta\) are: the squared "distance" \(ds^2=\sum g_{ij}d\theta^id\theta^j\) is, up to a factor, the Kullback–Leibler divergence between them, and the Cramér–Rao bound says no estimator can resolve \(\theta\) better than this distance allows. So the parameter space of a model is a Riemannian manifold, with curvature in general. The normal family with known variance, \(N(\mu,I)\), is flat Euclidean space. The normal family with unknown mean and variance, \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), has \(ds^2=(d\mu^2+2d\sigma^2)/\sigma^2\): a hyperbolic plane, the geometry of the upper half-plane in which the geodesics are semicircles, and in which there is "a lot of room at infinity" (its area grows exponentially with the radius).

The Jeffreys prior and the predictive density. The Riemannian volume \(\pi_J\propto\sqrt{\det g}\) is the Jeffreys prior, the natural "flat" prior of the model. Given \(N\) observations and a prior \(f\), the Bayesian predictive density is the posterior average of \(p(\cdot\mid\theta)\), and its quality is its Kullback–Leibler risk, the expected divergence from the true distribution of the next observation. A prior \(f\) asymptotically dominates \(\pi_J\) if its predictive density has smaller risk for every \(\theta\), to the first order in \(1/N^2\) where priors first matter.

Komaki's theorem (2006). Such a prior exists if and only if the Fisher manifold is non-parabolic, that is, carries a Green function, that is, its Brownian motion is transient. For the normal location model in \(d\) dimensions the manifold is flat \(\mathbb R^d\), the Green function exists only for \(d\ge3\), and the shrinkage prior it provides is Stein's harmonic prior \(|\mu|^{-(d-2)}\): Stein's threshold once more, this time for prediction. For the location–scale model the manifold is the hyperbolic plane, which is non-parabolic already in dimension two, so a shrinkage prior exists with just two parameters.

The appendix of the paper records what Komaki's risk expansion is, in the language of geometry. For a prior \(f=e^{-\Phi}\pi_J\), the pair \((g,\Phi)\) is a smooth metric measure space, the curved cousin of the weighted spaces of Part II, and Perelman (2002) attached to it a weighted scalar curvature \(R^{\mathrm m}_\Phi=R+2\Delta_g\Phi-|\nabla\Phi|_g^2\), with \(R\) the ordinary scalar curvature of the Fisher metric.

Proposition A.1

\[ \mathrm{Risk}_N(\pi_J)-\mathrm{Risk}_N(f) = \frac{R^{\mathrm m}_\Phi-R}{2N^2}+o(N^{-2}) = -\frac{2}{N^2}\,\frac{\Delta_g u}{u}+o(N^{-2}), \qquad u=e^{-\Phi/2}. \]

Hence \(f\) asymptotically dominates the Jeffreys prior if and only if \(u\) is a nonconstant positive superharmonic function, if and only if \(R^{\mathrm m}_\Phi\ge R\) with strict inequality somewhere.

The risk reduction of a prior is thus the \(\Phi\)-dependent part of Perelman's curvature, and the operator that governs domination is Perelman's \(-4\Delta+R\) with the curvature term removed. Proposition A.2 adds that the scalar curvature \(R\) itself has no statistical counterpart: the prior-independent part of the risk is made of embedding curvatures of the model (nonzero even for one-parameter families, where every intrinsic curvature vanishes), so curvature enters statistics through the recurrence of a diffusion, Brown's on the sample space and Komaki's on the parameter space, and not through a curvature term in a risk formula. Whether the scattered valleys of Theorem B have an analogue on a Fisher manifold, where Komaki's criterion would then separate the existence of a shrinkage prior from the growth of the model's spheres, is open.

Theorem AFor doubling + Poincaré priors, Brown's sphere test is necessary and sufficient for admissibility.A one-integral admissibility check for a large, natural class; and a guarantee that no geometric criterion can find a new admissible case there, so effort goes to the inadmissible side.

For a working statistician the practical content is this. If a prior's blur is regular (no sudden concentrations, no walls), compute one integral, Brown's sphere integral, and you know whether the rule can be beaten. If you want a rule that is "almost admissible" in every radial sense yet beatable, you now have an explicit recipe, and you know that the beatability is invisible to any test that looks along rays or averages over spheres. If your prior has walls, be careful: the ball-mass heuristic can point the wrong way, and the flux bound along the walls is the tool to use. For an analyst, the construction is an example of a doubling + Poincaré weight on \(\mathbb R^3\) with a prescribed combination of behaviours (cubic volume growth, divergent resistance along every ray, finite resistance overall), which may be of independent interest.

The honest scope. Theorem A is a short combination of known theorems. The genuine novelty is the construction of Theorem B together with the two-sided picture around it, and the care taken to make every weight a legitimate prior. The paper claims novelty only as "not found in the literature we searched"; a citation sweep is still to be done, and the comparison with Brown and Hwang's unified sufficient condition of 1982 is listed as open.

22. Open questions

  • Is there a characterisation of the undecided zone without doubling + Poincaré, perhaps through the flux test along a well-chosen exhaustion, which decided the wall?
  • The construction lives in \(\mathbb R^3\). In the plane the valleys would be points, transience needs volume growth faster than \(r^2\log r\), and the arithmetic of exponents changes.
  • Does the Brown–Hwang sufficient condition of 1982 cover the lattice prior? The paper expects not, but has not checked their hypotheses.
  • Do the scattered valleys have an analogue on a Fisher manifold, where Komaki's criterion would separate the existence of a shrinkage prior from the growth of the model's spheres?

23. Glossary and symbols

Admissible
An estimator that no other estimator beats at every \(\theta\). Inadmissible: one that some other estimator beats everywhere (weakly) and somewhere strictly.
Generalized Bayes estimator \(\delta_F\)
\(\delta_F(x)=x+\nabla\log f^*(x)\), the posterior mean under a prior \(F\) that may have infinite mass.
Gaussian blur, marginal \(f^*\)
\(f^*=\varphi*F\), the prior convolved with the standard normal density; the density of the observation when \(\theta\) is drawn from \(F\).
Weighted space \((\mathbb R^m,w\,dx)\)
Ordinary space with volume counted by \(\mu(A)=\int_Aw\,dx\). \(V(r)=\mu(B_r)\); \(\bar f(r)\) the sphere average of \(w\); \(A(r)\) the sphere average of \(1/w\); \(Q=\bar fA\) the spherical \(A_2\) ratio.
Weighted Brownian motion
The diffusion with generator \(\Delta+\nabla\log w\cdot\nabla=w^{-1}\operatorname{div}(w\nabla\,\cdot)\); a thermal particle in the potential \(-\log w\), or diffusion in a conductor of conductivity \(w\).
Energy, Dirichlet form \(\mathcal E(u)\)
\(\int|\nabla u|^2w\,dx\), the power dissipated by the voltage \(u\) in the conductor \(w\).
Recurrent / parabolic
The particle returns to every ball forever; equivalently \(\operatorname{cap}(B_1)=0\), infinite resistance to infinity, no Green function. Transient / non-parabolic is the opposite.
Capacity
\(\operatorname{cap}(B_1)=\inf\mathcal E(u)\) over \(u=1\) on \(B_1\) with bounded support: the effective conductance from the ball to infinity.
Sphere test, ray test
Brown's two radial tests: divergence of \(\int dr/(r^{m-1}\bar f)\) gives recurrence; bounded ray integrals \(\int dr/(r^{m-1}w(r\varphi))\) on a cone give transience.
Undecided zone
The priors on which both tests are silent: the sphere integral converges and no cone has bounded ray integrals.
Doubling
\(\mu(B(x,2\rho))\le D\,\mu(B(x,\rho))\) for all balls.
Poincaré inequality (scale-invariant)
\(\int_B|u-u_B|^2d\mu\le C_P\rho^2\int_B|\nabla u|^2d\mu\) for every ball of radius \(\rho\); best constant \(1/(\lambda_1\rho^2)\) with \(\lambda_1\) the first Neumann eigenvalue. "D+P": doubling and Poincaré together.
Heat kernel \(p_t(x,y)\)
Density of the particle at time \(t\); under D+P comparable to \(e^{-c|x-y|^2/t}/V(x,\sqrt t)\).
Volume test
\(\int^\infty r\,dr/V(r)=\infty\) implies recurrence; an equivalence under D+P.
Flux bound
\(R_{\mathrm{eff}}\ge\sum\text{thickness}/\int_Sw\,dA\) over disjoint layers of any nested family of closed surfaces.
Co-dimension
Dimension of the space minus dimension of the set: a surface in \(\mathbb R^3\) has co-dimension one and separates; a curve has co-dimension two and does not.
\(a\asymp b\), \(a\lesssim b\)
Comparable up to fixed positive constants; bounded by a constant multiple.
Lattice prior
The weight \(w=\big((1+d^2)/(1+|x|+d^2)\big)^{\alpha/2}\), \(d\) the distance to the circles of Section 16, \(\alpha\ge4\).
Fisher manifold, Jeffreys prior
The parameter space with the Fisher information as metric; the Jeffreys prior is its Riemannian volume.
